% Paragraph 1 -- PINN background and motivation for the specific PDE.
One-dimensional temperature diffusion is not only an ocean-scale
phenomenon: the same governing equation describes heat transport in
engineered water bodies such as swimming pools, where heating and cooling
account for a significant share of operating energy costs, and a fast,
differentiable surrogate for the temperature field is potentially useful
for energy-aware control. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs)
\citep{raissi2019pinn} solve forward and inverse partial differential
equation (PDE) problems of exactly this kind by embedding the governing
equation, boundary conditions, and (for inverse problems) observational
data directly into the training loss of a neural network surrogate. We
study this class of problem through a specific, well-characterized
instance: the seawater temperature diffusion equation of
\citet{han2026seawater}, under Dirichlet, Neumann, and Robin boundary
conditions, in both forward (diffusivity known) and inverse (diffusivity
estimated from sparse data) form. This equation is a useful benchmark
precisely because closed-form analytical solutions exist for all three
boundary-condition types, which lets us measure error against ground
truth exactly rather than against a numerical reference solution -- a
property we rely on throughout this paper and return to in
Section~\ref{sec:method}.

% Paragraph 2 -- QML hype + hybrid quantum-classical PINNs as a response.
Quantum machine learning (QML) has, in recent years, pushed to connect
itself to problems beyond textbook physics -- optimization, materials
discovery, and increasingly scientific and engineering simulation of
exactly the kind PINNs target. Hybrid quantum-classical PINNs
(QCPINN/QPINN), which replace part of the classical network with a
variational quantum circuit, are the concrete form this push has taken
for PDE-constrained learning: recent work reports that such hybrids match
or exceed classical PINN accuracy while using a fraction of the trainable
parameters \citep{farea2025qcpinn}. If this line of work is to matter for
applied surrogate-modeling settings like the energy-management example
above, rather than only for problems chosen because they are convenient
for a quantum circuit to represent, it needs to be evaluated with the
same accounting for training cost that a practical deployment would
require -- not accuracy or parameter count alone.

% Paragraph 3 -- the gap / research questions.
Despite growing enthusiasm for hybrid quantum-classical PINNs, we find that
efficiency in this literature is reported almost exclusively via parameter
count or number of training epochs, rarely via wall-clock time -- a
distinction that matters because variational quantum circuits are, in the
near term, executed via classical simulation whose per-step cost scales
with circuit width and depth (see Section~\ref{sec:related}). This raises
two questions that motivate the present study:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] On a benchmark PDE with exact analytical solutions,
    does a QCPINN built on a previously proposed circuit ansatz
    \citep{farea2025qcpinn} match classical PINN accuracy once wall-clock
    training time is accounted for, not just parameter count?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] If it does not, is the gap attributable to
    training hyperparameters (initialization, learning-rate schedule), or
    to the quantum circuit's structure (expressivity or trainability)?
\end{itemize}

% Paragraph 4 -- contributions, stated as a list.
We make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A controlled classical-vs-quantum comparison across six matched
    scenarios (three boundary-condition types $\times$ forward/inverse),
    with explicit wall-clock accounting alongside accuracy metrics
    (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
  \item A two-phase ablation that first rules out training hyperparameters
    as the cause of the quantum branch's underperformance, then isolates
    circuit structure (qubit count, depth) while directly measuring the
    circuit-only gradient norm -- a diagnostic largely absent from prior
    QCPINN evaluations, which infer trainability issues indirectly from
    accuracy curves or rely on purely theoretical gradient-decay bounds
    (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
  \item A discussion of why our findings diverge from prior QCPINN results
    obtained with a closely related circuit design, and what that implies
    for how QCPINN efficiency should be reported going forward
    (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section~\ref{sec:related}
surveys prior QCPINN work in detail; Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the
PDE, the classical and quantum architectures, and the training and
evaluation protocol; Section~\ref{sec:experiments} presents the main
classical-vs-quantum comparison and the two-phase ablation;
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets these results against prior work
and states limitations; Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
