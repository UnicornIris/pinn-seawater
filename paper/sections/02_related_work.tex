Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) \citep{raissi2019pinn} have become
a standard tool for solving forward and inverse PDE problems by embedding
the governing equations directly into the training loss. Motivated by
broader interest in quantum machine learning for scientific computing, a
growing line of work replaces part of the classical network with a
variational quantum circuit (VQC), typically in a hybrid architecture where
classical layers handle pre- and post-processing while the VQC provides the
trainable core -- an approach variously termed QPINN or QCPINN.

\citet{farea2025qcpinn} introduce QCPINN and evaluate it, alongside a
continuous-variable (qumode-based) variant that trains very poorly across
the board, on five forward-only PDE benchmarks with Dirichlet-type boundary
conditions: Helmholtz, a 2D lid-driven cavity flow, a 1D wave equation,
Klein-Gordon, and convection-diffusion. Their headline claim -- 10--30\% of
the trainable parameters of a classical PINN with comparable or better
accuracy -- holds for three of the five: a 4--64\% reduction in relative
$L_2$ error on Helmholtz, Klein-Gordon, and convection-diffusion. On the
remaining two, the quantum branch is \emph{less} accurate than the
classical baseline despite using far fewer parameters (cavity: 11--23\%
higher relative error across the three output fields; wave: 12\% higher),
which the authors state plainly rather than omit \citep[Sec.~8,
``Conclusion'']{farea2025qcpinn}. They also report a timing column absent
from the abstract: per-iteration wall-clock time is 15--40$\times$ that of
the classical baseline in every one of the five benchmarks, which they
attribute to the cost of simulating the circuit classically rather than to
the algorithm itself. Separately, their qubit-based circuit sweep tests
four named topologies (Alternate, Cascade, Cross-mesh, Layered) crossed
with two data-encoding schemes (amplitude, angle); the accuracy and timing
numbers above are for each benchmark's best-performing topology, most
often Cascade or Cross-mesh -- both of which use continuously-tunable
controlled-rotation entanglers (CRX/CRZ) rather than fixed CNOT gates,
which the authors argue is why those two topologies converge more reliably
than Alternate or Layered.

\citet{hqpinn2025flow} report a closely related pattern on a different
problem family: the compressible Euler equations, tested across a smooth
harmonic traveling wave, a non-harmonic solution with a moving contact
discontinuity, and a 2D transonic aerofoil flow with a shock. A quantum
branch whose functional form is an explicit truncated Fourier series
achieves the single best loss of any model on the harmonic case (an
order-of-magnitude improvement over the best classical network, using far
fewer parameters), but ``significantly underperformed'' on the non-harmonic
case and underperformed further still on the shock-dominated transonic
case, where the quantum branch is additionally under-parameterized. The
paper's own stated thesis is that harmonicity of the target solution, not
problem difficulty in general, is what determines whether the quantum
branch helps: ``quantum PINNs excel in harmonic regimes... due to the
Fourier structure of PQCs... [but] struggle to generalize to
discontinuities or shocks.'' On timing, wall-clock cost is reported only
for the transonic case, where per-epoch cost for the quantum configurations
ranges 17--82$\times$ that of the classical configurations tested (the
paper's own summary rounds this to ``up to 100$\times$''); a separate,
larger ``two orders of magnitude'' figure appearing earlier in the paper is
attributed to other authors' measurements, not to this paper's own
experiments. Notably, this paper does not use the term ``barren plateau''
anywhere; its account of trainability loss in larger quantum models is
framed instead as ``excessive expressibility,'' a distinct (if related)
diagnosis.

Read together, these two papers already show that the value of a quantum
branch in a hybrid PINN is problem-dependent rather than uniform -- within
a single paper's own benchmark suite, in both cases -- and that reporting
practices vary: parameter-count efficiency is reported everywhere, but
wall-clock or per-iteration time is reported only for a subset of
benchmarks in each paper (all five in \citealp{farea2025qcpinn}, only the
hardest of three in \citealp{hqpinn2025flow}), which limits how directly
either paper's timing claims generalize.

A third study applies the same qubit-based circuit family to a different
application domain: \citet{qcpinn2025reservoir} solve four reservoir-
engineering PDEs -- a linear pressure-diffusion equation, the nonlinear
Buckley-Leverett saturation-front equation, a linear convection-diffusion-
adsorption equation, and a fully coupled nonlinear two-phase pressure-
saturation system -- with the same three named circuit topologies
(Cascade, Cross-mesh, Alternate) as \citet{farea2025qcpinn}. This is not an
independent replication: the authors state directly that their circuit
implementation "is developed based on the open-source code by
\citet{farea2025qcpinn}," reusing the same architecture skeleton for a new
problem class rather than testing it from a separate implementation. Two
findings distinguish this study's claim from the parameter-efficiency
narrative associated with that lineage. First, parameter counts are
deliberately matched rather than reduced (e.g.\ 273 classical vs.\
279--283 quantum parameters in their simplest case) "to ensure fairness in
the comparison" -- the reported accuracy gain is thus an equal-budget
comparison, not a parameter-efficiency one, despite reusing the QCPINN
label. Second, the advantage is not uniform across topologies or
benchmarks: no single topology wins on all four PDEs, the authors
themselves note the Cross-mesh topology "does not consistently yield
superior results across all scenarios," and one benchmark's own figure
shows a quantum configuration's error matching or exceeding the classical
baseline's at a specific time step, even as the paper's overall framing is
positive. All four benchmarks are forward-only with Dirichlet and
Neumann-type (no-flow) boundary conditions -- no inverse problems, and no
Robin conditions. Notably, wall-clock or per-iteration training time is
not reported anywhere in this paper, not even as a partial column of the
kind \citet{farea2025qcpinn} and \citet{hqpinn2025flow} include for some
subset of their benchmarks -- the only acknowledgment of quantum
simulation cost is a single qualitative sentence noting that "quantum
simulation has inherent overhead on classical hardware," with no
accompanying measurement.

A fourth paper is worth a brief, separately-caveated mention rather than
treatment as a fourth equivalent data point.
\citet{hqcpinn2026hydro} propose a hybrid quantum-classical architecture,
independently designed rather than built on \citet{farea2025qcpinn}'s
implementation, and apply it to Sri Lankan flood data using the
Saint-Venant shallow-water equations. Despite the framing, the underlying
task is a four-class flood-severity \emph{classifier} with the PDE
residual used only as a loss regularizer, not a solution field scored
against ground truth -- so its headline ``71.8\% vs.\ 69.4\%'' comparison
is classification accuracy, not a relative-error metric, and is not
commensurable with the accuracy numbers reported by any of the other three
papers or by our own study. The paper's own results table also
contextualizes that comparison considerably: both physics-constrained
models trail the same authors' non-physics classical baselines (random
forest 90.3\%, Bayesian neural network 91.96\%) by roughly 20 points, sit
below what a majority-class classifier would score given a 91\% class
imbalance, and a transfer-learned variant of their own quantum model
outperforms the flagship configuration being compared (73.6\% vs.\ 71.8\%).
Like \citet{qcpinn2025reservoir}, this paper reports no wall-clock or
per-iteration timing anywhere, so its claimed ``2.2--3.6$\times$ fewer
epochs to converge'' cannot be checked against, and may not survive,
real training-time accounting -- a fourth instance of the same reporting
gap. Its barren-plateau-mitigation argument rests on a single gradient-
variance measurement at one fixed qubit count rather than the scaling
curve the underlying theory (which it does not connect to
\citealp{holmes2022expressibility}) would need to substantiate.

On the purely theoretical side, \citet{qpinnmac2025} propose a coupling
scheme between classical and quantum branches and prove that, under it,
the quantum-parameter gradient norm is bounded above by
$\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{\mathcal{N}N})$ in circuit depth $\mathcal{N}$ and
qubit count $N$ -- a polynomial rather than exponential decay -- with no
PDE experiments of any kind (the paper's own conclusion lists
``experimental validation on both ordinary and partial differential
equations'' as future work). Two caveats limit how much weight this result
can carry as context for our own findings. First, the architecture as
formulated handles ordinary, not partial, differential equations: its
network takes a single scalar input $t$, not the multiple independent
variables (e.g.\ our $(z,t)$) a PDE requires. Second, the specific
polynomial rate is obtained by first choosing the target approximation
tolerance to scale as $1/\sqrt{\mathcal{N}N}$ and then showing the
gradient bound matches that same rate, which reads as a property of that
choice rather than an emergent, ansatz-intrinsic result -- we cite the
qualitative conclusion (polynomial rather than exponential decay is
achievable under some coupling schemes) rather than the specific rate.

Our work differs from these four closely related studies in three ways.
First, three of the four restrict the quantum branch to forward
PDE-solving problems with Dirichlet and, at most, Neumann-type boundary
conditions (\citealp{farea2025qcpinn} and \citealp{qcpinn2025reservoir}
exclusively forward; \citealp{hqpinn2025flow} likewise), while the fourth
\citep{hqcpinn2026hydro} is not a PDE-solving comparison at all in the
sense used here; we test both forward and inverse formulations across
three boundary-condition types (Dirichlet, Neumann, Robin) on a single
diffusion PDE with closed-form solutions in all six cases, which lets us
measure error against ground truth exactly rather than against a numerical
or experimental reference, or a downstream classification label. Second,
wall-clock accounting is inconsistent or absent across all four:
\citet{farea2025qcpinn} report per-iteration time as one column among many,
\citet{hqpinn2025flow} report it for only one of three benchmarks, and
neither \citet{qcpinn2025reservoir} nor \citet{hqcpinn2026hydro} report any
timing measurement at all despite the former explicitly acknowledging that
simulation overhead exists and the latter reporting an epoch-count
``speedup'' with no way to check whether it survives real-time accounting;
we report wall-clock training time as a primary outcome for every scenario
alongside accuracy, and find the quantum branch uniformly worse on both
axes in all six -- a stronger and more consistent negative result than any
prior paper's own (partially mixed, in the case of the first two) findings.
Third, none of the four isolates circuit
structure as an experimental variable with direct gradient measurement:
\citet{farea2025qcpinn} discusses trainability differences between
entangler choices (CRX/CRZ vs.\ fixed CNOT) qualitatively, and formally
invokes barren plateaus only for its continuous-variable circuit, which is
not the qubit-based design we build on; \citet{hqpinn2025flow} attributes
reduced trainability in larger circuits to ``excessive expressibility''
based on final-loss curves; \citet{qcpinn2025reservoir} offers only
qualitative loss-curve comparisons and explicitly disclaims any causal
circuit-selection rule; \citet{hqcpinn2026hydro} offers a single gradient-
variance measurement at one fixed qubit count rather than the
qubit-count-scaling curve its own theoretical claim would need. We
instrument the circuit-only gradient norm directly across a controlled
qubit-count/depth sweep, providing an empirical data point none of the
four papers' diagnostic approaches supplies. We also note, for framing
rather than as a fourth point of contrast, that
\citeauthor{qcpinn2025reservoir}'s study is a direct architectural
descendant of \citeauthor{farea2025qcpinn}'s open-source implementation
rather than an independent replication; the convergent finding across it
and \citet{hqpinn2025flow} -- that a quantum branch's advantage, where
present, is topology- or regime-dependent rather than uniform -- is
nonetheless drawn from two separate author teams and problem domains.
